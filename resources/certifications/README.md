# Certifications: An Honest Guide

Certifications are a signalling mechanism, not a curriculum. They can open a first interview, satisfy a contract requirement, or prove a baseline to a non-technical hiring manager. They cannot replace a portfolio, and several of the most expensive ones teach less than free labs.

This page lists 24 certifications with provider, level, cost model, prerequisites, real relevance, and free preparation material. Prices change constantly and vary by region, so cost is described as a model rather than a number.

> Ethics reminder: never use exam dumps or leaked questions. They violate the provider's terms, devalue the credential for everyone, and leave you unprepared for the practical work. See [../../ETHICS.md](../../ETHICS.md).

## Certifications are optional

Read this before spending money.

- **No certification is required to start learning.** Every skill in this repository can be built with free labs and public documentation.
- **A certification is a filter, not a ranking.** Many hiring managers use one to shortlist; almost none hire on a certificate alone.
- **Hands-on certifications age better than multiple-choice ones.** A practical exam proves you can operate under time pressure; a memorisation exam proves you can memorise.
- **Employer reimbursement changes the calculus.** If your employer pays, take the practical one. If you pay yourself, start with the cheapest credential that matches the job you want.
- **Expensive does not mean respected.** Several premium-priced entry certificates are widely regarded as poor value; the table says so where that is the common view.
- **The best signal is still a portfolio.** Three documented engagements with reports beat any badge for a technical interviewer.

If a certification is the only way past a specific gate — a government contract, a partner requirement, a promotion rubric — treat it as a cost of doing business and get it efficiently. Otherwise, spend the money on lab time.

## Studying without buying a course

1. **Download the official exam objectives.** Every provider publishes a domain breakdown. It is the only syllabus that matters.
2. **Map each objective to a free lab or document.** If you cannot find one, the objective is probably vocabulary you can learn from the provider's own overview.
3. **Build a one-page cheat sheet per domain in your own words.** Copying someone else's sheet is a reading exercise, not a memory exercise.
4. **Practice the format.** Multiple-choice exams reward recall under time pressure; practical exams reward enumeration and reporting discipline. Train the format you will face.
5. **Schedule the exam before you feel ready.** A date converts vague study into a plan. Reschedule once if you must, not five times.

## What employers actually check

- Can you explain a finding to a non-technical stakeholder?
- Can you show work you have done, even in a lab?
- Do you understand the systems you claim to test?
- Will you stay inside scope and document what you did?

Certification gets you the conversation. These four questions decide the outcome.

## Cost planning

| Situation | Sensible approach |
| --- | --- |
| No budget, no employer support | Free labs and free courses first; consider one low-cost practical exam only when job hunting |
| Employer offers reimbursement | Choose a hands-on certification that matches the work you want to do |
| Contract or compliance requirement | Get the specific credential named in the contract, nothing more |
| Career change with limited funds | One practical entry credential plus a portfolio beats three multiple-choice badges |

## Integrity and professionalism

- **No dumps.** Using leaked questions breaches the candidate agreement and can void your certification.
- **No proxy exams.** Remote proctoring exists because people tried; the consequences are career-ending.
- **Quote the objective, not the dump.** When you study, cite public objectives and documentation.
- **Respect embargoes.** Do not publish detailed exam content; it harms candidates who follow the rules.
